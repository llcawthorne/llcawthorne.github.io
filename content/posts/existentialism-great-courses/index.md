I listened to two Great Courses lecture series on existentialism, *No Excuses:
Existentialism and the Meaning of Life* by Robert C. Solomon and *Existentialism
and the Authentic Life* by Skye C. Cleary.

*No Excuses* had a lot of lectures devoted to fictional works, more than I
expected from a philosophy course. The title comes from Sartre's idea of
Absolute Freedom, that even chained in a dungeon you're still free to choose
your own interpretation of the facts and how you react to them. The Bad Faith
concept stuck with me too, the idea that you're acting in bad faith any time
your aspirations ignore the actual facts of your situation. I could aspire to be
a champion basketball player, but the facts say I'm 5'11" and almost 50, so
aspiring to that would be bad faith.

*Existentialism and the Authentic Life* covered a wide variety of existentialist
authors I hadn't heard of before, along with the generally expected ones. My
favorite was already Camus, since I've read some of his works before, and his
idea of the Absurd, the tension between wanting the universe to have inherent
meaning and it flatly refusing to provide any. I'll admit my own notes on it
were thin though, since I was working out at the time, so I ended up going to
Gemini afterward to help me reconstruct the Myth of Sisyphus material properly.

Heidegger and Ortega were both phenomenologists that liked to make up words,
which is about the most accurate one-line summary I can give either of them.
When the lecture asked me to fill in the blank on "I am ___," I immediately
reached for "Senior Software Engineer," which was exactly the trap Sartre was
pointing at: you're always more than your vocation, no matter how many labels
you stack up.
