The first thing that struck me about *The Foundations of Western Civilization*
was how abnormally fast it moved before it got to Greek history. The biggest
Bronze Age civilizations got a lecture, and in five lectures or so we were in
Greece and things slowed down. For Egypt in particular, it felt like they summed
up the entirety of Egyptian history with a sentence or two each about the Old
Kingdom, Middle Kingdom, and New Kingdom, then talked a little about Egyptian
life and were done.

The lecturer's background in religious history really shows in his topics.
Religion got a lot of time, and not that it wasn't big in history, but the
course especially got bogged down comparing the Reformation and
Counter-Reformation over three lectures. I would much rather have had more
detail on Egypt than three lectures on the differences between Luther and Calvin
and the specifics of how the Catholics chose to counter them. But then again,
I'm an atheist, and every time the course touched on religion it reminded me how
long religion has held us back and distracted us with imaginary concerns. It
also spent a whole lecture each on the Hebrews, Jesus and the New Testament, the
Christian Church in Late Antiquity, Scholasticism in Monasteries, and Mohammed
and Islam. To think that theology was once the Queen of the Sciences and the
second most important subject taught in universities. It also felt like he
downplayed the Inquisition, just mentioning as an aside that it was a thing
while telling us so much else about the Catholic Church.

The lecturer also had a bone to pick with the way history is commonly taught.
His claim was that the Roman Empire never really had a Fall, except in Edward
Gibbon's imagination looking back from the 18th century. Likewise, there were no
Dark Ages. What we call the Dark Ages was a great time of expansion in Europe
and hundreds of years where it was happily not held back by plagues. He also
disagreed with the idea of the Renaissance, even though he gave a completely
acceptable description of what characterized it and how it started in Florence
and moved outward. Indeed, Charlemagne's kingdom was viewed as a new Rome by
contemporaries, even though it unfortunately didn't last more than a generation.

This is the first of two Western Civilization courses, and it went quite a bit
longer than I expected. I didn't expect much after Rome, but it covered the
Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Age of Exploration and Empire, so it was a
broad sweep that went all the way to the New World. It's kind of a fun point
that I didn't know Canute was a real king when I watched *Vinland Saga*, but
this series of lectures educated me on the Danelaw and the Danish conquest of
Britain.

A few ideas stuck with me along the way. The lectures framed Sparta's wars,
Athens' trade, and Corinth's colonies as responses to the same land shortage,
and while war and trade looked stronger at the time, I think colonization had
the biggest long-term impact, since that's what spread Greek culture. I also
noticed that Sparta and Rome were both devoted to ancestral custom (Lycurgus in
Sparta, the mos maiorum in Rome) and both deeply militaristic, though
innovative, democratic Athens was just as violent and just as willing to embrace
"might makes right," so tradition isn't the whole story. Rome's conquests were
often framed as defensive, since fear of Rome pushed its neighbors to aid Rome's
enemies, which gave Rome its next target, though historians debate whether
"defensive imperialism" is accurate or just Roman self-justification. And it's
remarkable that so many lasting political ideas came from Greece and Rome, which
were both prone to demagogues and strongmen, maybe because later thinkers
learned as much from their failures as from their successes.

The best thing I came away with is that progress often comes from holding a
nation to its founders' ideals. Abolitionists and suffragists succeeded largely
by demanding the nation live up to its stated principles, which is in a sense a
conservative argument in service of progress. The flip side is that people who
believe in change can be too sure they know how to steer it, which is a real
argument for caution, and it pairs well with Chesterton's fence from *The Scout
Mindset*.

I've picked out a good follow-up course that dedicates 48 lectures just to
Egypt, but I'm not sure where I'll fit it in.
